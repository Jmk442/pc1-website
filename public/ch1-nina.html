Script · The Inference Pipeline
You Press Enter. Then What?

You press Enter. That is your side of the interaction.

But what happens next — in the fraction of a second before a response appears — is a process most people never consider.

Your text is first broken into tokens. A token is roughly a word or part of a word — the sentence 'Tell me about climate' becomes approximately five tokens. Each token is converted into a numerical vector, a mathematical representation the model can process. This step is called tokenisation.

Those vectors are then passed through a transformer network — a series of computational layers, each one refining the model's understanding of what you asked and what should come next. The network does not look up an answer. It generates one, one token at a time, calculating the most probable next word given everything it has processed so far.

This is called autoregressive generation. And it runs at full computational cost for every single prompt — regardless of whether the question was clear or vague, structured or scattered.

Step 1: Tokenisation. Step 2: Embedding. Step 3: Forward pass through transformer layers. Step 4: Token generation and return.

Script · The Ambiguity Problem
Why Vague Prompts Cost More

Natural language is inherently ambiguous. A prompt like 'Write about the environment' activates probability distributions across multiple semantic domains simultaneously — ecology, policy, economics, geography, atmospheric science. The model must resolve this ambiguity probabilistically, producing outputs that attempt to cover all possible interpretations.

The consequence is measurable. Unstructured prompts generate an estimated three to four times more output tokens than structured equivalents delivering the same informational content. Longer outputs are more likely to miss the user's actual intent, triggering retry cycles.

Industry data suggests that users relying on unstructured prompts retry three to four times per task on average. Each retry runs the full inference pipeline at full cost — tokens, computation, generation, delivery. Nothing is cached. Nothing is reused.

The ambiguity in the original prompt does not reduce the cost of the retry. It multiplies it.

Script · The Scale Effect
From One Prompt to One City's Power

At the level of a single interaction, the numbers are small. Google has measured the cost of a median AI text prompt at 0.24 watt-hours of electricity, 0.03 grams of CO₂ equivalent, and 0.26 millilitres of water.

That is unremarkable. What is not unremarkable is the scale.

More than 200 million people use AI tools every day. Most retry three to four times per task when using unstructured prompts. That produces an estimated 600 to 800 million unnecessary prompts every single day.

At 0.24 watt-hours each, that is 144 to 192 megawatt-hours of wasted energy per day. Every day. Not from the prompts that worked. From the ones that didn't — because the question wasn't clear enough the first time.

This is not an abstract environmental concern. It is a direct consequence of how we communicate with AI systems.

Script · The Key Insight
The 32–48 Percent Reduction

Researchers have quantified what happens when structured prompting replaces unstructured prompting. Podder et al. (2026) demonstrated that structured prompt design reduces inference energy consumption by 32 to 48 percent across multiple large language models.

This is not achieved through hardware improvements. Not through switching to renewable energy. Not through regulatory mandates or capital investment.

It is achieved through education. Through structured communication.

The quality of how you ask determines how hard the machine has to work. A precise, well-structured prompt — one that tells the model what it needs to know before it begins generating — requires fewer calculations, fewer tokens, fewer retries.

That is what C.R.A.F.T. is. A structured method for communicating with AI systems. Five elements. One pipeline. And at scale, a measurable difference.
