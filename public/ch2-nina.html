Script · The Loop Nobody Talks About
The Loop Nobody Talks About

Across millions of AI interactions every day, a predictable pattern repeats.

Someone needs a document — a research summary, a report draft, a client brief. They type a prompt. The response is technically accurate, but misaligned: too long, the wrong angle, covering ground the user did not ask for. So they refine. They retry. They send a follow-up instruction: make it shorter, add more on this point, change the tone.

Three or four prompts later, they have something usable.

This is the retry loop. And it is not an edge case — it is the default behaviour for most AI users. Each iteration runs the full inference pipeline at full computational cost. The first prompt was not wasted on computation — it was wasted on ambiguity. And the retries that follow pay the same price.

The loop is invisible. But it is not small.

Script · The Numbers
What the Loop Costs at Scale

From Chapter 1: one AI text prompt uses 0.24 watt-hours of electricity and 0.26 millilitres of water.

Now scale that.

More than 200 million people use AI tools every day. Most of them retry three to four times per task when their first prompt does not return what they needed. That is 600 to 800 million unnecessary prompts per day.

At 0.24 watt-hours each, that is 144 to 192 megawatt-hours of wasted energy every single day. Eighteen to 24 tonnes of CO₂ equivalent. Up to 208,000 litres of water.

Annualised, the retry waste across the global AI user base represents approximately 52 to 70 gigawatt-hours per year — equivalent to the annual electricity consumption of a small city.

From prompts that did not work. From the absence of a structured question.

Script · The Email Parallel
A Literacy Problem We Have Seen Before

The AI prompt crisis is not unprecedented. It mirrors a pattern that emerged with the adoption of electronic mail.

When email became widespread, there were no established norms for how to use it. People wrote formal letters. They wrote rambling streams of consciousness. They wrote one-line messages that required six replies to resolve a simple question. The medium was new, and the conventions had not formed yet.

Eventually they did. Communication norms for email emerged — through workplace culture, through education, through practice. The quality of electronic communication improved. Not because email itself changed. Because people learned how to use it.

The same transition is happening now with AI. Most users have no framework for structured prompting. They type as they would type a search query, or a text message, or a verbal request. And the AI produces the most probable response to that ambiguous input — which frequently is not what they needed.

The gap is not technological. It is literacy.

🔄 Adapt — Structured Prompt Example

What a Structured Prompt Looks Like

Source: the World War 2 essay example from Year 7 version. ADAPT — replace with a universal workplace example (not Australian school assignment format).

Here is what the difference looks like in practice.

Unstructured: 'Write a summary of renewable energy trends.'

What you get: several paragraphs covering solar, wind, geothermal, hydroelectric, tidal — across multiple regions, at varying levels of technical depth. Probably not what you needed. You refine. You retry.

Structured: 'You are an energy analyst writing for a policy audience. Summarise the three most significant renewable energy trends in Europe over the last five years. Three paragraphs, formal tone, one key data point per trend.'

What you get: exactly that. First attempt. No retry.

Same topic. One question. The difference is structure.
