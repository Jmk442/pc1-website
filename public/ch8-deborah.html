Script · Opening
Same Information. Completely Different Feeling.

Same information. Completely different feeling.

Imagine a doctor telling you that you need a filling at the dentist. Now imagine three versions of that conversation:

  1. Friendly: "Hey, you've got a small cavity — nothing major! We'll get it sorted quickly and you'll be good as new."
  2. Formal: "The examination reveals a Class II cavity in tooth 36. I recommend a composite restoration at your earliest convenience."
  3. Scary: "You have decay in your tooth. If untreated, it could spread to adjacent teeth and may eventually require extraction."

Same fact. Same tooth. Three completely different experiences. The first makes you feel calm. The second makes you feel like you're reading a textbook. The third makes you want to run.

That's tone — the last letter in C.R.A.F.T., and the one that decides how your answer feels to the person reading it.

Script · Why Tone Matters
Why Tone Matters for AI

Because AI doesn't automatically know who's going to read its output. If you ask for a summary of climate change and you're writing for your Year 7 class, you need a tone that's clear, friendly, and encouraging. If the same summary is for a government report, you need a tone that's formal, precise, and evidence-based.

Without a tone instruction, AI picks a default voice — usually something that sounds like a polite Wikipedia article. It's not wrong, but it's rarely right for any specific audience.

And when the tone doesn't match? You rewrite it. Or you re-prompt. Either way, energy is spent fixing something that should have been right the first time.

🔄 Adapt — The Tone Spectrum Table

Six Tones — read as spoken list

The book has a tone spectrum table. Read the six tones as a spoken list — one sentence per tone. Pause between each.

Here are six tones and when to use them.

Friendly — sounds like a teacher explaining to a student. Best for school work, blogs, and emails to friends.

Formal — sounds like a lawyer writing a letter. Best for reports, applications, and official documents.

Persuasive — sounds like someone trying to convince you. Best for debates, advertisements, and opinion pieces.

Encouraging — sounds like a coach before a game. Best for motivational content, feedback, and instructions for beginners.

Neutral — sounds like a news reporter stating facts. Best for summaries, data descriptions, and factual overviews.

Technical — sounds like a scientist writing for other scientists. Best for research, specifications, and expert-to-expert communication.
Script · USE verbatim — Two-Tone Example
Same Content. Two Tones.

[Production Guide says USE — read both versions, the contrast is the lesson]

A real example — same content, two tones:

Friendly tone, for a Year 7 class: "Every time you ask AI a sloppy question, a computer somewhere burns electricity for nothing. It's like leaving every light in your house on and then complaining about the power bill."

Formal tone, for a government report: "Unstructured prompt inputs generate redundant inference cycles, contributing to measurable increases in per-query energy expenditure across large language model deployments."

Same message. Two audiences. If you sent the formal version to a Year 7 class, they'd stop reading. If you sent the friendly version to a government minister, it wouldn't be taken seriously. Tone makes the difference.

How tone completes the C.R.A.F.T. stack:

By the time you reach Tone, you've already set Context — the world. Role — the expert. Action — the task. Format — the shape. Tone is the final polish. It doesn't change what the AI says — it changes how it says it.

The right tone means the output fits its audience perfectly. The wrong tone means you either edit it yourself — time wasted — or re-prompt — energy wasted.
