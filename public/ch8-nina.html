Script · Register as the Final Constraint
Tone — The Last Element

Tone — more precisely, register — is the final element in C.R.A.F.T. because it operates on output that is already contextualised, role-informed, task-directed, and structurally defined.

Tone does not change the informational content of the output. It calibrates the linguistic register to match the intended audience.

Register calibration involves three dimensions. The first is vocabulary complexity — the density and domain-specificity of word choice. A patient-facing register uses common vocabulary; a clinical register uses technical terminology. The second is sentence structure — formal registers use longer sentences with subordinate clauses; accessible registers use shorter, active sentences. The third is hedging and authority — academic registers hedge, executive registers assert, educational registers simplify.

Without tone specification, the model defaults to a mid-register generalist voice. Technically competent. Not wrong. But not calibrated to any specific audience — which means it frequently satisfies none of them.

🔄 Adapt — Doctor/Dentist Analogy

Three Versions of the Same Information

Source: the three-version doctor/dentist scenario from Chapter 8. Keep the structure — adapt register for Year 11–12. All three versions work in audio.

Consider three ways to communicate the same clinical fact.

Friendly: 'You've got a small cavity — nothing serious. We'll sort it out quickly and you'll be fine.'

Formal: 'The examination reveals a Class II cavity in the lower right first molar. I recommend a composite resin restoration at your earliest convenience.'

Alarming: 'You have active decay in that tooth. If left untreated, it risks spreading to adjacent teeth and may eventually require extraction.'

Same tooth. Same decay. Three completely different experiences for the person receiving the information. Same content — different register, different emotional impact, different outcome.

This is what tone controls. And the AI needs to be told which one you want.

🔄 Adapt — Tone Spectrum Table

Six Tones — Read as Spoken List

Source: the Tone Spectrum table from Chapter 8. TABLE IS CONTENT — read as a spoken list: six tones, one sentence each.

Six points on the tone spectrum.

Friendly. Conversational, warm — suited to blogs, educational content, emails to colleagues.

Formal. Measured and precise — suited to reports, official documents, submissions.

Persuasive. Advocates a position — suited to opinion pieces, proposals, arguments.

Encouraging. Motivational, affirming — suited to instructions for beginners, feedback, coaching content.

Neutral. Objective, fact-stating — suited to summaries, factual overviews, reference material.

Technical. Expert-to-expert, no simplification — suited to research, specifications, professional documentation.

Six tones. Each one appropriate for a different purpose and audience. The model needs to know which one you need.

Script (USE) · Friendly vs Formal
Same Message — Two Registers

Here is a direct example from the manuscript — read both versions.

Friendly register: 'Every time you ask AI a sloppy question, a computer somewhere burns electricity for nothing. It is like leaving every light in your house on and then complaining about the power bill.'

Formal register: 'Unstructured prompt inputs generate redundant inference cycles, contributing to measurable increases in per-query energy expenditure across large language model deployments.'

Same message. Same underlying point. If the friendly version went to a policy committee, it would not be taken seriously. If the formal version went to a general audience, most of them would stop reading by the end of the first sentence.

Tone is not style. It is calibration. And miscalibration has a cost — either in credibility with the audience, or in the retry required to fix it.

🔄 Adapt — Institutional Tone Standards (Section 8.3)

Tone at Organisational Scale — Two Sentences

Source: Section 8.3 on institutional tone standards. Shorten to two spoken sentences maximum.

Organisations that deploy AI at scale produce content for multiple audiences simultaneously — internal teams, executives, regulators, and the public. Without tone standardisation, each user's prompt produces output in a different register, creating an inconsistent organisational voice that undermines credibility and requires additional editing at every level.
